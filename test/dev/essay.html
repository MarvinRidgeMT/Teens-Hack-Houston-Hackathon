Why the drought keeps coming back

Draft for Ms. Rivera · 1,180 words

Droughts in the region have grown longer over the past two decades. Reservoirs that once refilled every spring now stay low into the summer, and towns that never thought about water now post rules about when lawns can be watered.

The causes are not a mystery, but they are tangled. Warmer winters mean less snow, and less snow means less meltwater in the spring. Hotter summers pull more water out of the soil. Growing cities ask the same rivers for more every year.

What the numbers show

Rainfall has been below average since 2023, and the state's reservoirs were at about half of their usual level by the end of last summer. That gap matters most to farmers, who plan their planting a year ahead and cannot wait for a wet season that may not come.

Some farmers have already switched to crops that need less water, such as sorghum instead of corn. The switch is expensive and slow: new seed, new equipment and a few lean years while they learn what grows well on their land.

What a town can do

Cities have more options than farms. Fixing leaking pipes can save a surprising share of the water a city pumps. Paying people to replace thirsty lawns with native plants has worked in other dry states, and recycling wastewater for parks and golf courses keeps drinking water for drinking.

None of these fixes is enough alone. Together, they could make the next dry year an inconvenience instead of an emergency.

Conclusion

The drought keeps coming back because the climate that used to refill the region has changed. The question is no longer whether there will be another dry year, but whether we will be ready for it.
