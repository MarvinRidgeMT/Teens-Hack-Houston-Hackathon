# Calm canvas, one bright spark: colouring Pickup

Pickup should use a **calm, warm-neutral base with one vivid "spark" accent and a small set of meaning-coded colours**, not a fully saturated, Duolingo-style palette. The recommended direction is a warm off-white canvas (#FAF8F4) and soft charcoal ink (#1E2230), a deep-but-lively teal primary (#0D7C72, with a brighter #2DD4BF used as a fill behind dark text), a violet "resume" colour (#5B4BE0) for the pick-up-where-you-left-off moment, and apricot (#FFB547) reserved for rewards. Comparable apps split into two camps: gamified learning tools (Duolingo #58CC02, Quizlet #4255FF, Khan Academy #5753FA) that use bright saturated fills, and mindful focus and planning tools (Forest, Flow, Structured, Notion, Opal) that go muted, dark or near-monochrome with a single luminous accent. The research gives the reason for a middle path. The well-known hue claims ("blue boosts focus", "red hurts test scores") have mostly failed replication. What holds up is that **saturation and brightness, not hue, drive arousal**, so a focus tool should keep its large surfaces low-chroma and put its vibrancy into small, purposeful accents. Pickup's job is helping students return after distraction. The interface should therefore feel like a soft landing, never an alarm, and that rules out red for missed or abandoned tasks. Every pair in the recommended palette meets WCAG 2.2 AA when used in its stated role.

## Gamified learning apps shout; focus apps whisper with one accent

The comparable products sort cleanly by how loud they are. **Duolingo** is the loudest. Its core is Feather Green #58CC02 with Mask Green #89E219, Eel #4B4B4B text on Snow #FFFFFF, and a bold secondary set of Macaw #1CB0F6, Cardinal #FF4B4B, Bee #FFC800 and Fox #FF9600 used for UI, illustration and full-bleed backgrounds ([Rainbrand](https://www.rainbrand.com/brand-guidelines/duolingo/); [Canny Creative](https://www.canny-creative.com/atlas/brand/duolingo/)). Each colour has a job: green for primary buttons, red for errors and hearts, yellow for rewards, orange for the streak flame, blue for secondary actions ([Omoro](https://www.omoro.io/brands/duolingo)). **Quizlet** has moved from its older #4257B2 logo blue to a brighter indigo **#4255FF**, with teal #12C1CB, a cool off-white #F6F7FB background and #282E3E text ([quizlet.com](https://quizlet.com); [pickcoloronline](https://pickcoloronline.com/brands/quizlet/)). **Khan Academy's** live site uses blue-violet **#5753FA** on a #EBF1FD tint ([khanacademy.org](https://www.khanacademy.org)).

Productivity tools keep one saturated hue on a warm neutral base. **Todoist** pairs red **#E34432** with a warm off-white #FEFDFC and #25221E text ([todoist.com](https://www.todoist.com)). **Raycast's** official brand red is #FF6363 on a preferred #151515 dark background ([Raycast press kit](https://www.raycast.com/press)), yet its buttons are light grey. One design teardown says the red "is a brand mark, not a functional color" ([Refero Styles](https://styles.refero.design/style/3b6a17f0-3bdf-418c-a95e-0b89e5a8b2f8)). **Notion** is near-monochrome on its marketing site (#000000, #1A1A1A, #F6F5F4) ([notion.com](https://www.notion.com)). Its in-app tag colours are deliberately desaturated, for example Green #548164 on #EEF3ED and Blue #487CA5 on #E9F3F7 ([Matthias Frank](https://matthiasfrank.de/en/notion-colors/)).

Focus and wellbeing apps are the quietest. **Forest** is a near-black green #051A13 with cream #FCF6E4 text and a pale lime #E7FF9A highlight ([forestapp.cc](https://www.forestapp.cc)). **Opal** sets electric blue #0075FF against #040404 ([opal.so](https://www.opal.so)). **Flow** uses deep teal-green #267866 with sage #7CADA2 ([flow.app](https://www.flow.app)). **Structured** uses dusty slate blue #6385A8 with soft coral #FFA7A0 ([structured.app](https://structured.app)). The exception is pomodoro apps, which inherit the tomato: Focus To-Do uses #FF4837 ([focustodo.cn](https://www.focustodo.cn)) and Session uses raspberry #D13D50 with neon green #ADFF2F on #2B2B2B ([stayinsession.com](https://www.stayinsession.com)). In Pickup's direct category, browser extensions, the field is mostly bland. Toby uses hot pink #F65077 ([gettoby.com](https://www.gettoby.com)), OneTab uses generic #0073E6 blue ([one-tab.com](https://www.one-tab.com)), StayFocusd uses muted slate #5A6FA0 ([stayfocusd.com](https://www.stayfocusd.com)), and LeechBlock has no deliberate palette at all ([proginosko.com](https://www.proginosko.com/leechblock/)).

Most of these values come from observed marketing-site CSS, not official guidelines. Only Duolingo (through guideline mirrors), Raycast, Google Classroom and Notion's block colours have firmer documentation, and in-app colours for Forest, Opal, Tiimo and Structured were not checked. The pattern still holds: **green for growth and focus, red for timers and urgency, yellow or orange for rewards, blue-violet for learning and calm.** The 2025–2026 sites lean toward warm off-whites instead of pure white, or dark bases with one glowing accent. Blue-violet is crowded (Quizlet, Khan, Opal, Arc's #6E5DBF), and so is lime green (Duolingo). Teal paired with apricot is comparatively open.

## Colour psychology's famous hue effects mostly failed to replicate

Much "colour for focus" advice traces back to a few small priming studies. Mehta and Zhu's 2009 *Science* paper reported that red helped detail-oriented tasks and blue helped creative ones ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19197022/)). A replication with enough statistical power **did not reproduce the effect** ([Steele 2014, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24222366/)), and the FORRT replication atlas lists the finding as "replicated: failed" ([FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1169144/)). The claim that red impairs achievement ([Elliot et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17324089/)) fared no better. Gnambs's 2020 meta-analysis found **"no evidential value for an effect of red color on intellectual performance"** once publication bias was corrected, with adjusted effects near zero ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32696125/)). Even "green boosts creativity" ([Lichtenfeld et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22427383/)) was not confirmed when the original authors ran a "methodologically sound experiment" in 2018 that showed no effect ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10400419.2018.1488197)). Elliot's own colour-in-context theory says any effect depends on setting ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00368/full)), so lab results do not transfer directly to a browser extension.

The finding that does hold up is about intensity. Wilms and Oberfeld (2018) varied hue, saturation and brightness separately (N = 62) and measured skin conductance and heart rate as well as self-reports. **Saturated, bright colours raised arousal.** Saturation had a significant effect on skin conductance (ηp² = .18), but **hue had no significant effect** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28612080/); [author PDF](https://www.staff.uni-mainz.de/oberfeld/downloads/Wilms-Oberfeld2018_Article_ColorAndEmotionEffectsOfHueSat.pdf)). Brightness also raised pleasantness, and greys were liked less than chromatic colours. Pure grey austerity therefore has a cost too. Nielsen Norman Group draws the practical conclusion: there is "little real research proving a universal effect of a particular color on emotions" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/color-enhance-design/)). For Pickup, the hue choice is mainly about brand and meaning. How much of the screen is saturated is what affects calm.

The evidence has limits. It comes from brief lab primes on mostly adult, Western samples. No study was found that tests UI colour themes on students during real study sessions, or compares dark and light mode for sustained attention. ADHD guidance favouring muted palettes, few accents and limited motion ([Print Magazine](https://www.printmag.com/industry-perspectives/why-neurodivergent-ux-is-the-future/); [UXPA](https://uxpa.org/designing-for-adhd-in-ux/); [Tab-able](https://tab-able.co.uk/a11y-tip-designing-for-adhd/)) is practitioner consensus, not trial evidence. It is consistent with the arousal data, though, and costs little to follow.

## Meaning matters more than mood, and red should never mean "you slipped"

Colour does reliably carry convention. NN/g notes that **red is the colour most associated with errors**, orange and yellow with warnings, and green or blue with success ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/errors-forms-design-guidelines/)). This matters a lot for Pickup, because the product exists for the moment after a student has drifted off. If an abandoned task, a broken streak or an overdue session turns red, the interface treats a normal lapse as an error. That is the guilt dynamic critics see in Duolingo's streak, whose orange flame and icon changes many users describe as loss-aversion pressure ([Duolingo Help](https://www.duolingo.com/help/what-is-a-streak); [Deconstructor of Fun](https://duolingo.deconstructoroffun.com/mechanics/streaks)). No study has separated the effect of streak colour from the mechanic or the copy, so this is design judgment rather than tested evidence. The safer pattern is to show paused or missed states in neutral grey with gentle wording, and keep red only for real destructive actions such as deleting a task.

Rewards follow a well-established convention: warm yellow, orange and gold (Duolingo's Bee #FFC800 and Fox #FF9600, Khan Academy's gold streak highlight) ([Omoro](https://www.omoro.io/brands/duolingo); [DesignSystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/design-systems/khan-wonder-blocks)). A softer apricot fits Pickup better than flame orange. It also echoes WGSN's 2024 Apricot Crush. Trend signals point two ways at once. Gen Z's identity colours run screen-bright: Gen Z yellow, purple, then 2024's "brat" green ([The Conversation via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2025-09-millennial-pink-gen-yellow-brat.html)). Forecasters, meanwhile, have turned calm: Pantone chose Cloud Dancer white for 2026, described as a "calming influence" that "enhances our focus" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/12/04/nx-s1-5632651/pantones-color-of-the-year-2026-white)), and WGSN chose **Transformative Teal** for 2026, a blend of "dependable dark blue and aquatic green" signalling "freshness, calm, and restoration" ([WGSN](https://www.wgsn.com/en/blog/colour-year-2026-transformative-teal)). Both are marketing forecasts, not preference data. They still suggest that a warm-white base with teal and violet accents will look current to students without feeling loud. The authors of the Conversation piece warn that generational colours are cultural and change quickly. User-selectable accent themes are a cheap way to cope with that.

## Vibrant and accessible means saturated fills behind dark text, not saturated text

The comparable apps show two ways to keep bright colours readable. One is a saturated fill with white text for the primary button and a pale tint with brand-coloured text for secondary actions (Quizlet #EDEFFF/#4255FF, Khan #EBF1FD/#5753FA, Google #E8F0FE/#1967D2) ([quizlet.com](https://quizlet.com); [khanacademy.org](https://www.khanacademy.org); [edu.google.com](https://edu.google.com/workspace-for-education/classroom/)). The other keeps the brand colour off functional text altogether, as Raycast and Opal do with neutral buttons ([raycast.com](https://www.raycast.com); [opal.so](https://www.opal.so)). The bar is WCAG 2.2: **4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text and UI components**, and colour must never be the only signal ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). APCA models perception better, but its place in WCAG 3 was still undecided as of April 2026, so the practical advice is to meet WCAG 2 and use APCA as a second check in dark mode ([Adrian Roselli](http://adrianroselli.com/2026/04/wcag3-contrast-as-of-april-2026.html)).

Contrast ratios calculated for this report with the WCAG 2 formula show the risk of brightness. **White on Duolingo's #58CC02 is only 2.09:1**, white on Macaw #1CB0F6 is 2.44:1, and Bee yellow #FFC800 on white is 1.55:1. All three fail as text pairings, which is why Duolingo uses these colours as fills and icons. Quizlet's #4255FF (5.28:1 with white) and Khan's #5753FA (5.22:1) pass. Their indigo is about as bright a hue as can carry white text. Neon accents work only on dark grounds: Forest's #E7FF9A on #051A13 reaches 16.47:1 and Session's #ADFF2F on #2B2B2B reaches 11.54:1. Dark modes in the best systems are re-tuned, not inverted. Notion lifts Green #548164 to #4F9768 on #242B26 ([Matthias Frank](https://matthiasfrank.de/en/notion-colors/)). Khan Academy added darker ramp stops and adjusted hues for contrast on dark surfaces ([Khan Academy Blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/how-we-rebuilt-khan-academys-color-system-from-the-ground-up/)). Raycast specifies #151515, not pure black ([Raycast](https://www.raycast.com/press)). About 1 in 12 men have red-green colour vision deficiency ([NEI](https://www.nei.nih.gov/learn-about-eye-health/eye-conditions-and-diseases/color-blindness)), so timer and task states also need icons or labels, not just a red/green difference.

## Recommended direction for Pickup: "Warm Desk, Teal Spark"

The proposal follows NN/g's 60-30-10 structure ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/color-enhance-design/)). Neutrals cover 60–90% of the surface, which keeps arousal low. Teal is the brand and action colour. Violet marks Pickup's signature "resume" moment, so returning to a task looks distinct and welcoming. Apricot appears only at celebration moments. Teal and violet together are distinct from Duolingo's green, Todoist's red and the blue-violet that Quizlet and Khan occupy, and teal matches the 2026 calm-restoration trend. All ratios below were computed for this report.

| Role | Light mode | Dark mode | Use rule and contrast |
|---|---|---|---|
| Canvas | #FAF8F4 warm white | #14171F soft charcoal | Not pure white or pure black |
| Surface / card | #FFFFFF | #1C2029 | Popup and panels |
| Ink (body text) | #1E2230 | #E6E8EE | 14.9:1 / 14.6:1 |
| Muted text | #5C6170 | #9AA0AE | 5.8:1 / 6.8:1 |
| Primary teal (buttons, links, progress ring) | #0D7C72 | #3DD6C4 | White on #0D7C72 5.07:1; #3DD6C4 on dark 9.9:1 |
| Teal tint (secondary buttons) | #E3F4F1 bg + #0B6E65 text | n/a | 5.38:1 |
| Spark teal (highlights, celebration fills) | #2DD4BF fill + #1E2230 text | #3DD6C4 | 8.5:1; never as text or thin lines on light (1.75:1) |
| Resume violet ("pick up where you left off") | #5B4BE0; tint #EEEBFF | #A99CFF | 5.95:1 on white; 5.09:1 on tint; 7.6:1 on dark |
| Reward apricot (streaks, milestones) | #FFB547 fill + #1E2230 text | #FFC46B | 9.0:1 / 11.4:1; fills and icons only |
| Paused / missed (neutral, not red) | #8A8F9C icons + "rest" copy | #9AA0AE | 3.05:1, meets non-text 3:1; use muted ink for text |
| Destructive only (delete) | #B42318 | #F97066 | 6.57:1 / 6.43:1 |

Several implementation rules make this palette work. Saturated colour goes into small areas: one primary button per view, the progress ring, the resume card's edge or tint. Large surfaces stay neutral. Bright teal and apricot always sit behind dark ink and never act as text on light backgrounds. Every state pairs colour with an icon and a label. Celebrations stay under five seconds, can be skipped, never flash, and respect `prefers-reduced-motion` ([Tab-able](https://tab-able.co.uk/a11y-tip-designing-for-adhd/); [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). Offer a "Calm" theme that tones the accents down further, and optionally one or two student-chosen accent swaps (violet-led, lime-led) to absorb shifting trend taste. Treat the hex values as a starting point to validate with real students and a colour-blindness simulator. They are not the result of user testing.

## Conclusion

For Pickup, the question is less which colour helps focus and more where colour is allowed to appear. The hue research that colour-psychology marketing relies on has largely collapsed. What remains (intensity drives arousal, red means error, warm colours mean reward) is enough to set rules for placement and restraint, and it leaves hue free for brand differentiation. The strongest opportunity is emotional: Pickup's most important screen is the one a student sees right after getting distracted. A palette that marks that moment in a welcoming violet instead of a warning red, and saves celebration for actual progress, makes colour part of the product's anti-guilt promise rather than decoration. The main open question is empirical. No study yet shows how students respond to these choices in real study sessions, so a small A/B test of the "resume" treatment (violet versus neutral) on return rate would tell Pickup more than any further literature review.
